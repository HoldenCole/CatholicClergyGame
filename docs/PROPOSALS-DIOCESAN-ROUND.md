# A diocesan life, played: twenty proposals

*Written from a full diocesan playthrough (seed `dio-life-1`, Chicago, a son of the diocese from a working-class parish, widowed mother, entering at 22 in 2010; forty-nine years a priest, retired at 78) run through the engine year by year and then walked in the browser at seven points of the life: ordination, the second year as vicar, the first pastorate, twelve years, twenty-five, thirty-eight, and the shelf at the end. Nothing here is built. The owner's brief was ten quality-of-life improvements and ten things that deepen the play, with relationships and reputations that carry through the whole life as the spine. The bugs the playthrough turned up were fixed in the same round and are listed at the end.*

## 1. What the life looked like

The shape of it, from the yearly log:

- **Years 0–3**, parochial vicar at the cathedral. Parishioners 34 → 100 in three years, chancery in the forties, brother priests 9 → 55. Twelve people mattered (regard past ±15), all of them from the seminary years: two formators, both parents, the home pastor, a classmate.
- **Years 4–10**, three side postings (the penitentiary, the Newman Center, and a study leave), then pastor at 11. During the postings the scene draw thinned to two or three scenes a year and parishioners sat at 20.
- **Years 11–24**, two pastorates and the superintendent's office. A new bishop at 16 took the chancery from 56 to −6 in a year: the §5.3 reversal, and the one time in forty-nine years the numbers turned against him. By year 22 five pastors of the deanery, the rector, the home pastor, and four classmates were all at exactly 100, and stayed there for twenty-seven years.
- **Years 25–49**, one parish, St. Joseph in Wilmette, held as pastor, then as administrator of "a cluster of three churches" that turned out to be the same building, then as pastor again. Parishioners 99, chancery in the forties, brother priests in the seventies, every year the same. The traditional bloc went from +50 to −42 and the progressive from −32 to +56 across these years without a single scene noticing that the man had changed sides. The vicar for clergy reached −100 at year 28 and a group leader at −100 at year 31, and both stayed there, one of them running a thriving group in his parish. The board moved a man of 67 and then 73 to "a parish nobody asked for."
- **The shelf** listed ten friends, nine of them pinned at 100 and the tenth dead; two enemies; six bishops of whom the first was wrongly named the one who ordained him; and a file whose last ten lines were the same two offers declined over and over.

Two of the proposals below (D1 and D8) come straight from that shape: regard has no resting point, so it saturates, and the last quarter of the life is a plateau nothing moves.

## 2. Quality of life

**Q1. A People sheet for the whole life.** The only place the game lists who matters to the man is the shelf at the end. A live sheet, grouped as the shelf groups them (family, the class, the brothers, the chancery, the bishops, the people of every parish he has held), each with since when, last contact, the word for the regard, and the one line of history that explains it (the bonds, a favour asked, a quarrel, a direction refused). The engine already keeps everything this needs; it is a view. It is also the sheet every depth proposal below would land on.

**Q2. Letters that do not hide the desk.** The year in review and the bishop's letters sit over every sheet until they are put in the drawer, so the People, Deanery, and You sheets are unreadable during the one week the player most wants to read them (the review names the people carrying something and the openings ahead). Let a letter fold to a tray on a tab change and unfold on return, and keep the last five reviews readable on the Record sheet.

**Q3. An offer declined twice stops coming.** "A Room for an Old Priest" was offered thirteen times over thirty years, "A Summer on Loan" twelve, "In the Bishop's Place" nine, each two years after the last refusal. Second refusal closes the offer for good, with a line in the file ("the chancery has stopped asking"), and the letter gets a third answer beside accept and not-now: never.

**Q4. The review's "the parish says" line from the year that was.** "The parish says that you are a hard man to find outside Mass" appeared in the review at two years, twelve, and thirty-eight. Derive the line from where the hours actually went that year (the obligation left at minimum, the group starved, the visits not made), keep a pool per cause, and never say the same thing two years running.

**Q5. Captions with a name that identifies.** The scene modal captions each figure with a first name only ("Casey"), which is ambiguous in a presbyterate of Caseys and told the QA that a friar had wandered into the see when the man was Msgr. Casey Zawadzki, the vicar general. Title and surname, with the role on hover.

**Q6. The board says why it is looking, and stops looking at the old.** The review's "the board looks at you within the year" line runs to the end of the life, and the board moved a man of 67 and 73 to parishes nobody asked for. Past sixty-five the board leaves a pastor where he is unless he writes; the review says so in words ("the board has stopped moving men your age"); the Jobs sheet says what the board would weigh if he did write.

**Q7. Mail triage.** The mailbag is a flat list of "left in the drawer." Show unanswered letters as a count on the Letters tab, keep an unanswered letter in the tray for four weeks before it goes to the drawer, and never drop a letter from family silently: the mother's letter unanswered is a line in the review and a cost to that regard.

**Q8. The Record filtered by a person.** The Record sheet filters by kind (money, parish, people, diocese). Add a filter by person, so the life can be read through one relationship: every week that mentions Fr. Ibarra, from the seminary corridor to his funeral. The people are already in the text; this is an index over it.

**Q9. Skip-ahead stops for people.** The interrupt settings stop the clock by scene category. Add stops for people: a named person dies, leaves, or is moved; a letter from a named person; a bishop named; a classmate's life opening (drinking, ill, thinking of leaving). The engine writes all of these into the career and the lives; the clock only has to look.

**Q10. The deanery sheet with its history.** Now that the deanery refills its seats (fixed this round), show each seat with the years the man has held it and since when the player has known him, the dean's succession as a short list, and the cooldown on "have a word" in weeks instead of a greyed button.

## 3. Deepening the play: relationships and reputations that carry

**D1. Regard that settles.** Every relationship is one number moved by events and hours, clamped at ±100, and once there it stays: nine friends at exactly 100 for a quarter century, a vicar for clergy and a group leader at −100 for two decades. Give each relationship a resting point set by shared history (the bonds, the favours asked and granted, the years side by side in a deanery or a house, the quarrels, a direction refused), let regard drift toward the resting point when nothing is done, and make the top of the scale cost continued contact. The word on the sheet then means something: "a friend, since 2019, drifting" against "a friend, kept." `brothers.ts` already does the drift for classmates; this makes it the rule for everyone, with the history deciding the floor and ceiling rather than a constant.

**D2. The file follows him.** The chancery's file is the thing a priest's reputation actually is, and the game has it only as `chancery`, a number reset when a bishop is replaced. Make the file an object: dated entries written by officials and bishops (the vicar for clergy's note after a refused direction, the chancellor's after the lawsuit, the bishop's after the column, the summer evaluations of him as a seminarian), kept across successions. A new bishop reads the file, not the man: his opening regard is the file read through his own temperament, and the record's "how the bishop reads it" rows come from the entries. The player sees part of it on the You sheet and can learn the rest through a friend at the chancery (a `brothers` favour), or wait for the scene where it is read back to him. Officials outlive bishops, and so do their grudges: the vicar for clergy who sat at −100 for twenty-five years would then be writing.

**D3. Bishops who remember.** Six bishops in this life, and each one vanished on retirement. A bishop emeritus is a person: a letter from him a year on, his word to the nuncio when the man's name comes up years later (the ternas already consult), his funeral with the man asked to preach or pointedly not, and the bishop who ordained him at the silver jubilee. The shelf's list of bishops would then read as a set of relationships that ran on after the chair changed hands, which is what they are.

**D4. The class as a network that pays.** Two classmates became bishops elsewhere, one ended in the chancery, three left, and the four at 100 did nothing with it for thirty years. Extend the favours in `brothers.ts` by what the man has become: a classmate bishop can name him vicar general or offer a pastorate in his see (an offer through the offer engine, evaluated against state); a classmate in the chancery tells him what the board will do a season early; a classmate who left asks something only a priest can do (his daughter's wedding, a reference, a funeral for his father) and the answer moves the regard and the record. A class reunion every five years as a scene that reads the whole class's state aloud, so the player feels the drift.

**D5. The men he formed, grown.** Twenty-two summer seminarians evaluated over the life, each with a verdict, and none of them came back except as a curate for a summer. A man formed carries the verdict written on him: strong men come back as his vicar, his dean, his successor at the parish, the bishop's secretary; a man he wrote up as concerned comes back as the priest who remembers it; one of them, late, is named his bishop. Regard seeded from the verdict and the years since; scenes for the first meeting after.

**D6. Former parishes that remember.** Parishioners carry over in part on transfer and then the parish is gone from the game. Keep a standing per former parish and let it act: a family he bonded with asks him back for the funeral or the wedding (a letter through the mailbag, a weekend away, the regard and the bond); the parish's anniversary; a former parishioner turning up in the new parish; the parish he turned around going under after he left, heard as talk in the deanery; the parish he left badly writing to the bishop when his name comes up for a see nearby. The bonds are already on the people; this gives the people a reason to write.

**D7. Sides, and the cost of changing them.** The blocs flipped over twenty years and nobody said a word. Consistency is a derived value in the design (§5.5) with nothing hanging on it. Blocs remember: crossing from one to the other over years fires "the bloc that lost you" scenes (the old friend from the Latin Mass circle at the door, the columnist who quotes his own homily back to him), the You sheet carries a standing in words ("a man who changed, once" against "a man who never moved"), and each succession re-reads the record as a scene in which the man answers for it: owns it, explains it, or denies it, with the consistency cost of the third.

**D8. The last decade as its own phase.** Years 25–49 were one parish and one set of numbers. At sixty-five the game should change register: the board stops moving him; the younger men's regard becomes a thing he can see (the deanery's view of the old man, warm or tired of him); the retirement letter at seventy-five is a negotiation with the bishop rather than a dice roll (stay on, pastor emeritus in residence at a former parish where the relationships come back, the chaplaincy nobody else wants, the chancery's senior priest); the funeral he plans and the question of who will bury him answered from the relationships he kept, so that the shelf can say who came. The material for this is entirely the relationship history the life has already written.

**D9. The family across the life.** The mother and father have arcs (a parent dying, the widowed mother's illness) and then the family ends. Add the next generation: a sibling's children the man baptizes and later marries, the family Christmas as a scene that recurs across decades with the cast changing, the house when the mother dies (sold, kept, the sibling who wanted it), a sibling's divorce or a nephew who enters the seminary and telephones him from the fire escape in February. Family regard feeds the shelf and the last decade (D8): the family is who is there at the end for a man without a wife.

**D10. Enemies with agency.** Hostility is a number with no behaviour: a vicar for clergy at −100 and a group leader at −100 did nothing for twenty years. An enemy acts on his position: the official writes the file entries (D2), the lay leader organizes the petition to the chancery or takes her group to the parish across town, the classmate seeds the rumour in `talk.ts`, the pastor of the deanery speaks against him when the terna is drawn. Each enemy has a reason the player can learn (the record, a quarrel, a direction, a favour refused) and a way to mend it or not: an amends scene, a funeral attended, a letter answered. Enemies then carry through the life as the friends do, and are worth the hours to turn.

## 4. Fixed in this round

Found by the playthrough, fixed before this document was written:

- **The board appointing a man to an abstract opening.** An opening "across the diocese" (no parish behind it) could be won by the player, who was then appointed "pastor of A parish nobody asked for" and left exactly where he was, as administrator of his own parish. The board now names a real parish when he wins one: never the one he is leaving, never the cathedral, a vacant one first.
- **The Deanery sheet crashing.** When a pastor of the deanery was moved by the yearly churn, the sheet looked up his parish and found none. The deanery now refills its seats from the parishes' current pastors each week, a dean gone is succeeded by the senior pastor left (with a line in the record), and the sheet skips a man without a parish.
- **"Whose father in the car park you undefined."** The scenes write bonds of kinds the table had no verb for (taught, heard, told, formed) and one in a short form (`buried_him`). The verbs are there now and the short form is read.
- **The wrong bishop named as the one who ordained him.** The shelf called the bishop of his seminary years the one who ordained him even when that bishop had retired before the ordination. It now names the bishop in office at ordination.
- **The file on the shelf full of declined offers.** The ending's file and summary skipped nothing, so the last ten lines of a life were "Declined: A Room for an Old Priest." Declined offers stay in the record and leave the shelf.

## 5. As built

The owner asked for all twenty, five at a time.

**Batch 1** (D1, Q1, Q3, Q4, Q5).

- **D1 Regard that settles** (`systems/regard.ts`). Every person carries `marks` (a scene, a favour, a direction that moved the regard past a threshold, with the week and the why), a `contactWeek`, and a `friendSince`. The resting point is computed from who they are (`REGARD.base` by role: family 35, a classmate 8, a formator 5, the rest 0), the marks (half of each, capped at ±40), the bonds (4 each, up to 24) and the quarrels (−6 each), and stays within ±70. Each week without contact (eight weeks after the last hours, word, or scene), regard drifts toward the resting point at 0.06; above 70 it falls at 0.14, so the top of the scale costs keeping. Scenes write marks and contact through `noteMarks` in `applyChoice` (the sealed forum included, since regard is not reputation); the brothers' hours, a favour asked, and a word on a sheet count as contact. The brothers' own drift is gone into this. Numbers invented.
- **Q1 The Circle** (`systems/circle.ts`, `ui/CirclePanel.tsx`). A sheet on every desk: everyone with regard past ±15, a bond, a mark, a bishop, or family, grouped as the shelf groups them, with the word for the regard and what is happening to it ("a friend, since 2019, drifting"), when he was last in touch, and the last mark or the bonds as a line of history. A view; nothing stored.
- **Q3 Offers that stop.** Refused twice, a letter does not come again (`OFFERS.declinesBeforeClosed`); a second letter carries "Not again", which closes it at the first no and writes "the chancery has stopped asking" in the file.
- **Q4 What the parish says** (`content/parish/review_said.json`). The review's line comes from the year's routine: the homily from the file, no visits, the box at minimum, the homily at minimum, the preparation, the meetings, the groups left alone, then the care band; a pool per cause, chosen by the year and never the same line two years running (`review:said`).
- **Q5 Captions.** A figure in a scene is captioned with title and surname, the role on hover.

**Batch 2** (D2, Q6, Q2, Q7, Q10).

- **D2 The file** (`systems/file.ts`, `GameState.file`). Dated entries, each with who wrote it, what it says, a weight (−3..+3) and a lean, kept across successions and trimmed to the last eighty. Writers: the rector's yearly evaluation; the board's decisions (promotions and passings-over); stands taken aloud (the chancellor keeps the clippings, with the lean of the stand); a letter asking to be moved, and its outcome; an offer declined for good; a group leader's complaint to the chancery; talk that reached the bishop; and each year, every official of the chancery cold enough (−30) or warm enough (+40) toward him writes a note. Scenes can write one through the `file` effect. A new bishop reads the file in `revalue`: each note for him or against him by its lean against his own side, at 2.5 a unit, capped at ±20, added to the swing; his letter says how many notes and which way they cut. The man sees on the You sheet what was said to his face (the board, the rector, his own stands, his letters) and how many notes he has not seen; the brothers' "what he is hearing" favour now reads the last three out. Officials outlive bishops, and so do their notes.
- **Q6 The board and age.** Past sixty-five (`CAREER.boardLeavesAt`) a pastor with no letter on file stays where he is; the board's decisions are not taken; the review's "Ahead" line and the Jobs sheet say so, and say that a letter would move him.
- **Q2 Letters that fold.** A letter on the desk can be folded away for now: a tray line above the desk says it waits, the sheets are readable, and it unfolds on its button or when the next letter comes. The last five years in review stay readable on the Record sheet.
- **Q7 Mail triage.** A letter from someone can be answered later: it goes to the Letters sheet with its replies, counted on the tab, and the drawer takes it after four weeks. A family letter left in the drawer, then or on the spot, is remembered by the family (−6 and a mark). The review has a row for the letters that asked and were not answered, the family's by name.
- **Q10 The deanery's history.** The deanery keeps the week each man took his seat and the deans in order; the sheet shows since when each man has sat and the succession of deans; a cooling "have a word" says how many weeks.
